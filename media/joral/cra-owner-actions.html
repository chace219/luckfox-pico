01 · The short version

Why any of this is compulsory

The Cyber Resilience Act is an EU regulation. That word matters: it binds directly in every member state, with no national law to wait for and no transition period once a date passes. It applies to us as the manufacturer of a product with digital elements, and it applies to units we have already sold as well as to units we ship next.

It carries two dates that concern Joral.

11 Sep
2026

We must be able to report

If a weakness in our product is being actively exploited, or a serious security incident happens, we must tell the European authorities within 24 hours of finding out, with fuller detail at 72 hours and a final report inside two weeks. We must tell affected customers too.

11 Dec
2027

Everything else

CE marking, the technical file, the published security policy, and the security properties the product itself must have. Those properties are already built and demonstrated; that date is about paperwork and marking.

The September date is not a target we set. It is a clock, and we do not control what starts it.

Enforcement sits with national market-surveillance authorities, whose powers extend to ordering a product off the market, and the penalties for these particular obligations sit in the regulation's top band. Your counsel should confirm the figure — the point here is only that it is not a fine you absorb as a cost of doing business.

Separately from the regulation, a second deadline runs in parallel: the first customer unit. A handful of decisions become permanent the moment one leaves the building, because no software update can reach back and change them. Those are marked below.

3

Name who answers it, and who covers them

Required by 11 SepOwner: youA decision

Two names: who reads that mailbox on a working day, and who covers holidays and absence. Give them standing authority to file the 24-hour notice without waiting for you — that first notice commits Joral to no finding, it only says we are aware and looking.

Why it is compulsoryThe regulation sets the clock and is silent on who runs it. A 24-hour obligation with no named person is the most common way a company misses one.
4

Sign the firmware signing policy

Blocks first shipmentOwner: youA signature

Our units accept a firmware update only if it is properly signed. That works and has been proven on real hardware. But today it is signed with a temporary development key that lives on a working computer, and a unit that trusts that key is a unit whose update path we cannot vouch for. The policy that fixes it is drafted, recommended, and waiting for your name.

Why it is compulsoryThe regulation requires that security updates reach the product securely. That is met by the mechanism — and undone by where the key lives.
5

Confirm the support period: five years

Required — and blocks first shipmentOwner: youSame signature

We must state publicly how long we will provide security updates. The recommendation is five years from the date each model first goes on sale, with the end date printed. Five is the floor for industrial equipment; ten would not be honest today, because parts of the software foundation we build on are supported by their own makers for less than that. Extending later is always allowed. Shortening is a breach.

Why it blocks shipmentThe clock starts with the first customer unit, and the manual that ships with it is supposed to name the end date. A number nobody has agreed cannot be printed.

03 · The ceremony

The hour, and why it is shaped like that

Every unit we ship will check a signature before it installs firmware. The key that makes those signatures is therefore the most valuable thing Joral will own:

Whoever holds that key can put software on every unit we ever ship.

Which is why it is not created on a build server, or kept in a password manager, or held by one person. Four ideas shape the hour, and each one answers a specific way this goes wrong elsewhere:

  • It is made on a computer with no network, ever. A key that has never touched a connected machine cannot be taken from one. The laptop runs a temporary system that leaves nothing behind when it is switched off.
  • Two keys are made, not one. One is used; one is a spare that signs nothing. Both are trusted by every unit from the first one built. If the working key is ever compromised, we switch to the spare with an ordinary update — instead of recalling hardware, which is the alternative and is not really an alternative.
  • Two people, and both hold the passphrase. Neither of you can act alone, and neither of you leaving strands the company.
  • Four copies, in two buildings. One set in the office safe, one off-site. Losing a copy is an inconvenience; losing every copy would mean we could never update a fielded unit again.

What actually happens, and what is yours

I bring and run

  • A prepared start-up USB and the ceremony script, checked in advance
  • The four storage devices, ready to use
  • The commands themselves, on your workstation — nothing is installed on it, nothing is left behind, and it is disconnected from the network for the duration
  • The paperwork afterwards: the certificates go into the build, and every unit from then on trusts them

Yours, in the room

  • Be present for the whole hour — the procedure requires two people and you are one of them
  • Choose the passphrase with me, and remember it; it is never written into any system
  • Take custody of the two sets afterwards: safe and off-site, the same day
  • Sign the one-page record: the date, who was present, and which devices hold what

The signed record is not a formality. When an assessor asks how Joral controls the ability to install software on its products, that sheet of paper is the answer, and it is the kind of evidence that is very hard to produce after the fact.

Afterwards

Signing a release takes about fifteen minutes and involves one of the storage devices coming out of the safe, being used on a disconnected laptop, and going back. It is not a burden on your week; it is the one moment per release where the key is in use, deliberately kept slow and physical.

04 · Not yours

What you are not being asked for

So the list above reads as complete rather than as the first instalment.

  • The product itself is done. All eight security properties the regulation requires of the product are implemented, and each has been demonstrated on real hardware rather than in a test suite: secure settings out of the box, no known unfixed vulnerabilities at shipment, protected passwords and configuration, a stripped-down attack surface, access control, a security log, signed updates, and factory reset.
  • The evidence is being kept as we go. Every release carries a list of the software inside it, checked against public vulnerability databases, and archived. That is what lets us answer “which units are affected?” within the first hour of a report instead of the first week.
  • The remaining engineering is scheduled and blocks nothing — a test needing equipment we do not have here, two short bench checks, and the first full run of the automated attack-input testing that now runs nightly.
  • CE marking and the declaration of conformity are yours and counsel's, on the 2027 timeline, and they are not on this page because nothing about them is due yet. Engineering's job is that every fact those documents rest on is true and provable, and that part is done and evidenced.
